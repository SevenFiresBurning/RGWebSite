What Music Publishing Actually Means

A Resistance & Ground Guide for Independent Artists

For independent artists, music publishing is often where confusion becomes expensive.

The terminology is dense. The royalty system is fragmented. Different organizations handle different rights. And the word publishing can describe anything from basic administration to giving another party an ownership interest in your songs.

Resistance & Ground starts from a simple principle:

Artists should understand what they own before deciding what to give away.

That does not mean every publishing deal is bad.

It means ownership, administration, licensing, and collection are different things—and artists should know the difference.

The Song and the Recording Are Not the Same Thing

When a band records a song, there are generally two separate assets involved.

The composition is the song itself: the melody, lyrics, and underlying musical work.

The master recording is the specific recorded performance of that song.

Those rights can be owned by completely different people.

A band may own its masters while individual members own the compositions.

A label may control a recording while the writers retain their publishing.

A publisher may administer the song without owning or controlling the master.

That distinction matters because different uses generate different rights, licenses, and royalty streams.

If you do not know which asset you are talking about, it is easy to sign away more than you intended.

What Music Publishing Actually Is

Music publishing is the business surrounding the ownership, administration, licensing, and commercial use of songs.

Depending on the relationship, a publisher or administrator may help with:

  • songwriter and ownership records;
  • song splits;
  • PRO registration;
  • mechanical-rights registration;
  • publishing metadata;
  • royalty administration;
  • licensing requests;
  • sync preparation;
  • catalog organization;
  • song pitching;
  • and tracking where songs have been placed, submitted, or licensed.

For an independent artist, the first job is often not finding a giant publishing company.

It is making sure the catalog itself is sound.

Ownership and Administration Are Different

This is one of the most important distinctions in music publishing.

You can have someone manage your publishing without giving them ownership of your songs.

Publishing Administration

In an administration relationship, the songwriter or band generally retains ownership.

The administrator handles agreed publishing functions in exchange for a fee or percentage of publishing income.

The simplest way to understand it is:

You own the song.
They manage the business around it.

That can include registrations, metadata, royalty collection, licensing coordination, catalog organization, and pitching.

The exact agreement still matters. Artists should understand the term, territory, rights granted, income covered, commission, termination language, and any post-term collection period.

But administration itself does not have to mean surrendering ownership.

Co-Publishing Is Different

A co-publishing agreement involves ownership.

Under that structure, the publisher receives an agreed interest in the publishing rights covered by the deal.

That can make sense when the publisher is contributing substantially more than administrative work.

A true publishing partner may be:

  • actively developing writers;
  • creating co-writing opportunities;
  • securing artist cuts;
  • pursuing sync placements;
  • investing money or resources;
  • building licensing relationships;
  • or creating long-term commercial opportunities for the catalog.

The distinction is straightforward:

Administration is a service relationship.

Co-publishing is an ownership relationship.

That difference should be understood before percentages are ever discussed.

Song Splits Are Not Optional Housekeeping

If more than one person writes a song, the ownership should be documented.

Preferably before the song earns money.

A split sheet can include:

  • song title;
  • legal names;
  • professional names;
  • ownership percentages;
  • PRO affiliations;
  • IPI or CAE numbers;
  • publisher information;
  • and signatures confirming the agreement.

Nobody wants to argue about who wrote what after a licensing request arrives.

A song with unclear ownership may still be a great song.

It is simply harder to use commercially.

Distribution Is Not Publishing

Getting a song onto Spotify, Apple Music, or another platform does not mean every right connected to that song has been handled.

A distributor primarily deals with the recording side of the release.

Publishing concerns the underlying composition.

Likewise, registering a song with one organization does not necessarily mean every relevant royalty stream has been addressed.

There are multiple systems involved in modern music rights.

That is why the important question is not:

"Did we register the song somewhere?"

It is:

"Is the song properly documented and registered everywhere it needs to be?"

Metadata Is Part of the Work

Metadata is easy to dismiss until inaccurate metadata prevents money from reaching the right person.

Every serious catalog should maintain reliable information about each song.

That may include:

  • title;
  • alternate titles;
  • writers;
  • ownership percentages;
  • publishers;
  • PRO affiliations;
  • IPI/CAE numbers;
  • ISRC;
  • UPC;
  • release date;
  • master ownership;
  • lyrics;
  • recording versions;
  • instrumental versions;
  • clean versions;
  • stems;
  • and licensing information.

The goal is simple.

If somebody asks about a song, you should be able to answer quickly:

Who wrote it?

Who owns it?

Who controls the publishing?

Who owns the master?

Who can approve a license?

Build the Catalog Before You Need It

Artists often begin organizing rights information after something goes wrong.

A better approach is to do it before the opportunity arrives.

That matters especially with sync licensing.

Film, television, advertising, games, documentaries, and other media often require quick answers.

If somebody wants to license a song, they may need:

  • ownership confirmation;
  • writer splits;
  • master-control information;
  • lyrics;
  • an instrumental;
  • a clean version;
  • stems;
  • and confirmation that all necessary parties can approve the use.

The easier a song is to clear, the easier it is to license.

Great music deserves better than being passed over because nobody knows who owns it.

Publishing Should Do More Than Collect

There are two sides to publishing.

The first is administration.

That asks:

Are the rights documented correctly?

Are the songs registered?

Are royalties being collected?

The second is exploitation and development.

That asks:

What else can these songs do?

That may include:

  • sync licensing;
  • artist cuts;
  • cover opportunities;
  • independent film;
  • advertising;
  • games;
  • branded media;
  • regional licensing;
  • or other catalog opportunities.

Collection protects existing value.

Development creates new value.

A serious publishing strategy should understand both.

Not Every Artist Needs to Give Away Publishing

A band does not need a publishing deal simply because publishing exists.

Some artists can manage their own catalog effectively.

Others need administrative help.

Others may eventually benefit from a deeper publishing partnership.

The important question is not:

"Who should own our publishing?"

It is:

"What does the catalog actually need?"

If the songs are organized, ownership is documented, registrations are accurate, royalties are being collected, licensing materials are ready, and somebody is actively pursuing opportunities, the catalog is being managed.

If none of that is happening, handing away ownership may not be the first solution.

Getting organized may be.

A Better Starting Point

For independent artists, a limited administration arrangement can be a sensible first step.

Start with a group of songs.

Audit the catalog.

Identify what is missing.

Document ownership.

Correct registrations.

Build licensing assets.

Track royalty sources.

Prepare the songs for pitching.

Then evaluate the relationship based on actual results.

That gives both sides information before either side makes a larger commitment.

Ownership should not be surrendered simply because the business side of music feels confusing.

Confusion is not a reason to give something away.

It is a reason to understand it.

The Resistance & Ground Approach

Resistance & Ground exists to help independent artists build stronger foundations around the music they already own.

That means understanding the catalog before exploiting it.

Protecting ownership while creating opportunities.

Separating administration from ownership.

Keeping clear records.

Making songs easier to license.

And giving artists enough information to make deliberate decisions about their work.

The goal is not simply to collect royalties.

The goal is to turn a collection of songs into a functioning catalog.

One where you can answer:

Who wrote it?

Who owns it?

Because control begins with knowing what you have.

Know the song.
